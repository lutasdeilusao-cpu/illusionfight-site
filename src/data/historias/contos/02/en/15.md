# CHAPTER 15 — Marked

While the kids were growing up, my life was turning over.

I was sixteen with two years of running a neighborhood under my belt. I'd revitalized half a dozen things, organized what was a mess, made the money go up every month without fail. From the council's point of view, I was one of the best investments the Banca had ever made.

From the point of view of everybody I'd stepped over to get there, I was an unpaid debt.

---

You don't climb the way I climbed without leaving a trail.

Sombra had family. The Baixada that split had people who picked up the pieces and spent two years figuring out who'd started it. The collectors I squeezed, the managers I screwed over, the neighboring bosses who lost their edges to my plan — every one of them kept my name.

And, unlike me, they weren't in any hurry.

There came a month when the council called me up top and said it straight: you've become too big a target to stay. Either you disappear, or we lose you and the neighborhood with you.

I didn't argue. That wasn't punishment. It was math. The same math the Banca did when it left me as fodder in the reformatory — only this time I was the asset, not the throwaway, and you protect an asset.

I spent three weeks closing everything down. Handed the neighborhood to two lieutenants I trusted, split down the middle so neither of them got too strong and started getting ideas. Burned what needed burning. Said goodbye to nobody, because goodbyes leave a trail.

---

They already had it all set up.

Exit route. New papers. Another part of Marélia waiting for me — more trouble, farther away, and exactly because of that, safer, because nobody looks for a man who's gone down when they think he's gone up.

I was going to disappear. Forever, is what I thought. A guy hunted the way I was going to be doesn't go back to where he came from.

Back then I didn't know I was going to come back. But that part is way down the line.

---

Before I left, I only wanted one thing.

The last first Monday. The last fight with those two.

Thinking coldly, I'd made a mistake. I should've recruited both of them the first time they showed up — fought once, offered them the Banca, done. That would've been the right thing. It would've been useful.

But in that first fight against the two of them together, two years before, I felt something I'd never felt in a fight. And I didn't want to give that up too fast.

That's why I let it become a ceremony. That's why I let it last two years.

And that's why, before leaving that neighborhood and turning into a name on a wanted list, I was going to climb the Hill on a Monday morning and fight the Kid and the green hair one more time.

Me at sixteen. Them at nine.

The farewell.

And I was going to tell them. Face to face, before the first punch. "This is the last one."

Not out of kindness. Out of respect. Two years of taking a beating from me every first Monday without fail earns you the right to know when it's the last time. And, if I was honest with myself, I wanted them to know too. I wanted that fight to weigh on both of them as much as it was going to weigh on me.
