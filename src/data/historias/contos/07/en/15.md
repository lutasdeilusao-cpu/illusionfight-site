# CHAPTER 15 — The King's Tax

**ALAN**

— Wake up.

Nothing.

— Wake up.

He opened one eye. Then the other. He tried to move his arms and found out they were tied to the chair. He tried his legs and found out the same thing.

— What the fuck is this? What's going on?

I let him look around. A room with no windows, a single bulb, the smell of wet cement. My men leaning against the wall, in no hurry. Me sitting in front of him, legs crossed, waiting.

I always let them look around. Saves on explaining.

---

— So, my friend. You know who I am?

He stared at me. It took a while. His face still had the marks from that night — crooked nose, black eye, a sloppy bandage over the eyebrow. But what changed in his face wasn't any of that. It was the moment he understood.

— You… you're Alan. The king of Marélia.

— Exactly.

I uncrossed my legs.

— So if you know who I am, you should know the rules for doing business in my castle.

---

I don't know if you get why I'm the king of Marélia. I'm not wasting time explaining. I'll keep it short and clear.

Marélia is my castle.

Nothing happens here without me hearing about it. No bike rolls through, no load comes down, no warehouse opens its doors at night without somebody, somewhere, telling me. I knew about their club before it had a name.

— If you knew the rules of Marélia — I said —, you'd know you made a very serious mistake. Very serious.

---

He started talking fast. It's always like that. Whoever realizes he's done starts talking fast, like the words could pay for something.

— Man, I'm sorry. I'm sorry. We were just trying to set up our operation, that's all. If it's money, if it's product, we'll settle it, we'll split it, you can keep all of it. Only… only it all burned. All of it. Two lunatics showed up in our spot and wrecked everything.

Two lunatics.

I almost smiled.

---

— Here's the thing, sweetheart.

I stood up. Walked slowly around his chair.

— I don't care about your money. I don't care about your drugs. I don't care about your schemes.

I stopped behind him. He couldn't see me, and that made him shake harder.

— I already knew about all of that. I was letting it happen. Letting you grow. Because sooner or later you'd have to share with me, if you wanted to keep working here. Everybody shares. It's the king's tax.

I came back around to face him.

— But that part is negotiation. That part is just business. And business I settle with a conversation.

— Then let's talk, man. Please.

— Business isn't the problem.

---

— The problem is you broke a rule. A very clear rule.

I crouched down until I was at his eye level.

— There's one rule in this city. And it goes like this: the green-haired kid and the Kid in the blue cap, nobody touches.

He blinked. He didn't get it.

— That rule is clear to everybody who works in this city. From the smallest corner in the Baixada to the top of the Hill. Nobody lays a finger on a single hair of those boys.

— But… but who are those kids? Why'd they come after us?

— Doesn't matter why.

— Man, they set everything on fire. They…

— I don't know why. — I said it slowly, so he'd hear every word. — You must have done something really fucked up. I've seen those two work a lot. A whole lot. But I've never seen them do anything like what they did to you.

I looked at his bandage.

— You're lucky you walked out of that warehouse alive.

---

— Man, I run that place. — His voice cracked in the middle. — I didn't know. I swear I didn't know. If you wanted to send your men…

— They're not my men.

He stopped.

I think that was the first thing I said that really scared him. Because if those two weren't mine, and I was still there because of them, then they were something else. Something he didn't have a name for.

Neither do I. I've been trying for years.

— I don't know why they did it — he said, close to tears.

— Doesn't matter anymore. You already broke the rule.

I straightened my back.

— And when somebody breaks the rule in this city, they pay in blood.

---

He still tried something else. A name, a number, a promise. I wasn't listening anymore.

— We already picked up your whole crew that ran — I said. — The ones on the highways, the ones hiding at their mothers' houses, the ones out in the countryside thinking Marélia ends at the city-limits sign. It doesn't.

The ones the cops arrested that night wouldn't need me. I've got people in every jail in this state. They'd die in there, one by one, on their own time.

The ones who ran were in the warehouse next door, waiting.

A week from then, the whole club wouldn't exist anymore. Not the name. Not the jackets. Not even the memory that somebody once tried to build something in Marélia without paying the tax.

One hundred percent. So people learn to follow the rules.

---

I nodded to my lieutenants.

I didn't stay to watch. I never do. A king who gets his hands dirty with everything has no time left to be king.

I walked out of the room. The door closed behind me.

The rest was quick.

---

Outside, my right-hand man fell in beside me.

Same one as always. The one who grabbed me by the arm six years earlier, on a rooftop, with a revolver in his hand and a kid knocked out on the ground. He never forgot that night. Neither did I.

— Alan. — He lit a cigarette. — You're letting those kids go too far.

— I know.

— One of these days they're gonna mess with the Banca, you hear me? And the Banca's gonna collect. It won't be some roadside biker club. It'll be the Banca.

— Don't worry about it, kid.

— I do worry. Worrying's my job.

I stopped in the middle of the yard and looked at him.

— I've said it before and I'll say it again. Nobody messes with those two. Whatever comes down because of them, I'll take it.

He let the smoke out slowly. He didn't argue. He never argues when I use that tone.

---

— Well — he said, after a while. — At least this time they helped us out.

— They did.

— It's gonna make a good example. There's a bunch of little side hustles popping up on the down-low. People thinking they can grow without coming up the Hill to talk.

— Yeah.

I looked at the city down below. The lights coming on one by one.

Somewhere down there, in a hospital, a green-haired kid was telling the cops it had been a group motorcycle accident. And the other one, the Kid, was sitting next to Helena's bed, not talking to anybody.

Neither of them was ever going to hear about that room. Better that way.

---

— This time Kim and Jack were good for something — I said. — Instead of just bringing me problems, they brought me a solution.

My right-hand man laughed through his nose.

— It'll be a good warning — I added. — For everybody thinking about starting a side business in this city without paying the king's tax first.

I threw my coat over my shoulder and went down the stairs.

There was another thirty-something man waiting in my office. Two months behind.
