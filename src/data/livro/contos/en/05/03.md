# CHAPTER 3 — Uncle and Aunt

I'll call them Uncle and Aunt. The real names I threw out on purpose and I'm not going to dig them out of the trash now just to make the telling pretty.

I lived in that house from two to five. Three years. In a kid's head, three years is a whole lifetime.

---

The first thing that hit me was the noise.

Kids. Lots of kids. That wasn't a house — it was an old two-story pile, full of rooms, with a hallway that creaked and a staircase nobody trusted. Almost an orphanage, except with nobody even pretending they were going to take care of you. I don't know how many kids lived there. More than you could count in your head, and the number kept changing.

I was the youngest when I got there. I grew up in the middle of it.

---

It took me a while to figure out the scheme, because I was too little. But it got clear.

Every kid in that house was money at the end of the month. Every head was worth a cut of aid, of benefits, of help for a "large family". Uncle and Aunt had set up a little livestock operation of small humans, and we were the herd.

The money that was supposed to feed us came in and evaporated. Went into their pockets, into booze, into whatever. We got the bare minimum to stay on our feet — because a kid who's too skinny draws attention, and drawing attention was bad for business.

---

Food there was rationed like it was controlled medication.

There was a time, a portion, an owner. Going without was normal. I learned things at that empty table that I still carry: that water fools hunger for about forty minutes, that you can sleep with your stomach hurting if you curl up the right way, that the trash from certain places has usable stuff if you get there before the others.

Heavy curriculum for someone who couldn't count to ten yet.

---

And there was the beating.

I won't give details, because beating details are boring to read, and you've already seen me take a beating from Brock in that annex ([main line, ch. 3](/historias/lutas-de-ilusao/capitulo-03)). Take that, remove the crowd, remove the comeback, shrink it down to a three-, four-year-old, and multiply by the number of days. There. You've got the picture without me drawing it.

The worst part wasn't even the pain. It was that you couldn't predict it. A good day and a bad day wore the same clothes, and you only found out which was which when it was already too late. That messes with your head in a way the pain alone doesn't.

---

We weren't kids in that house. We were numbers.

Nobody knew my birthday. Nobody knew what I liked. I was a line on a spreadsheet, and my job was to keep breathing so I wouldn't ruin the count on the day the caseworker showed up — then, sure, there was decent food on the table, and Aunt talked in a soap-opera voice, and everybody smiled for the photo.

The caseworker left and the food left with them.

---

And there was the basement.

By day we were numbers for the photo. By night there was the basement, with a door that stayed locked while the sun was up. When it got dark, it opened, and the older kids went down. Over time the younger ones went down too. I went down.

Down there we worked. Little plastic bags, a scale, and a smell that grabbed your throat and never let go. I was four, five, I didn't know the name of it. But I knew the smell, and I knew the faces of the men who came by to pick up what we packed — men who looked at a child the same way they looked at a chair.

Uncle and Aunt weren't the top of anything. They were a link. Free labor, no payroll, no questions, and if a kid disappeared from there nobody was going to look. We packed what we were told to pack, the way we were told, and at the end of the night we went back up. Nobody explained what it was. We already knew it was the kind of thing you don't talk about.

It was there, in that basement, that crime showed itself to me for the first time in my life. Not as an opportunity. As a locked door and a smell in the throat.

---

There was an older boy who looked after the younger ones. Nobody told him to — he did what he could to soften the blows. Warned us when things were about to get bad. Shared the little he managed to hide.

One day he wasn't there anymore. "Went to another uncle." Nobody explained and nobody asked, because we'd already learned not to.

I think about him now and then. I hope it worked out. That's the most I can do for him from here.

---

Now the good part. There is one, and in this chapter it's the most important in the whole story.

In those three years I got, for free, the one thing I do better than Kim.

I learned to read the punch before the person throwing it knows it's coming.

I memorized the sound of the gate. The way Uncle stepped when the day had gone badly. The tone of Aunt's voice on the phone, which already gave away the whole next hour. The gap between the key going into the lock and the door opening. Every little signal like that I catalogued without knowing I was cataloguing, because the information was worth something concrete: not getting hit.

You think my read on a fight is a gift. It's not. It's trauma with good packaging. Those two gave me a training no money can buy, and I paid for it up front, on the spot, in childhood.

---

One night I came up from the basement, wedged myself behind the water tank in the yard, and stayed there until dawn.

It was cold. I'd stopped crying about that kind of thing — crying didn't move the score. And that night I understood something with a clarity that scared me, for the age I was: it wasn't the hunger that was going to finish me off there, or the beating. Hunger I could take. It was the basement. That house wasn't just cruel — it was feeding something bigger and worse out there, and whoever stayed was going to get swallowed with it, one little bag at a time.

I stared at the sky going gray and thought a line way too big for a kid:

*This isn't forever. Somebody decides when it ends. And it's going to be me.*

It wasn't a plan. It was a line. But I stored it alongside my mother's off-key voice, in the same little box.

Not long after that I turned five. And I made the first real decision of my life — to leave. Not just to run from the beating. To get away from that thing before it turned into me.
