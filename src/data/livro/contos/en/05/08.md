# CHAPTER 8 — Nobody Ever Asked

The envelope went to the backcountry.

To my parents. The same ones who handed me over at two because there was no food at home. I send them money every month, have for ages, and nobody in the world knows this but the three of us.

Hold on, I'll explain how we found each other again. And I warn you: the first part is ridiculous.

---

It was by letter.

LETTER. You get me? Paper. Envelope. Stamp. In an age where I've got a phone in my pocket that does more math than NASA, my connection to my parents was, for years, a LETTER.

Here's how it worked. Back at the aunt and uncle's house, a letter came now and then from my parents. Aunt almost never handed it over, but I'd grab the sender's address off the corner of the envelope and memorized it — told you that earlier. It was an address out in the backcountry, and it stayed burned into me.

When I got settled on the street, the first letter was one I sent. I wrote crooked, because I barely knew how to write, to the address I'd carried by heart since I was four. And I sent along, in big letters, where they should write back.

From there it turned into back and forth. Every time I changed spots — and I changed a bunch, from the gap to Mr. Nestor's shop, from the shop to the rented house — I'd send a letter first with the new address. They wrote it down and started writing there.

Weeks of waiting between one and the next. The reply always came more crooked than mine, because my mother writes worse than I do and my father just signs his name at the end.

---

The content of the letters was as follows: nothing.

"It rained last week, thank God." "Your cousin got married." "Are you eating right?" "The goat had a kid." Page after page of absolutely nothing happening, written with an affection that didn't fit on the paper.

I keep all of them. I won't say where.

---

Then I started making some money, and my patience with the last century ran out.

I bought an old phone, one of the cheap ones, and sent it to the backcountry. I set it all up first: saved my number as "SON", in big letters, because I know my clientele. And I pay that phone's plan every month to this day — because if it's up to the two of them to top it up themselves, we lose contact and go back to the mail.

Now we do video calls. Once in a while. The image freezes, the audio lags, my mother holds the phone a hand's width from her face and I spend the whole call staring at one giant eye and the wall behind her.

It's the best thing about my week.

---

My parents worry about me in a way that has no size.

Every call is an interrogation. What did you eat. How many hours did you sleep. Are you dressed warm. Who are these people you run with. Is this Kim guy really trustworthy. Are you studying. Are you getting into trouble — and there I lie, obviously, because "what trouble, Mom, come on" goes down better than the truth.

They lost almost all of my childhood. So now they want every detail, and I give the details you can give. I edit a lot. But I give.

---

The money I send every month.

It's a good chunk of what I make — always around sixty percent. It's enough for my father and my mother not to need to work anymore: for fixing the house, for buying medicine, food, and even a little luxury if they feel like it. Even so, the two of them keep working the same as ever.

The forty percent that's left, here in Marelia, for a street kid like me, is way more than I need. And beyond the stalls and the deliveries, I've still got the jobs me and Kim do together — each guy's hustle is his own, but there are the duo ones. And on those I never forget to set aside my cut ([like the bet that shoved Kim into LDI — main line, ch. 3](/livro/capitulo-03)). Yeah. Don't toy with my greed.

I keep asking my parents to come to Marelia. I tell them I'll take care of the two of them, that nothing will be missing. No use. They're country people to the bone, from another time. They don't want luxury, they don't want lights in their faces. They have little and they're happy that way, honest like you barely find anymore. Marelia would spoil them. Maybe it's better this way.

But I feel like I owe them.

It's that I was loved for two years, for real, and you take care of what's yours. They let go of me once, by force, with an empty pocket. I'm not going to let go back, on purpose, with a full one.

---

I've never seen them in person. Not that I remember.

I saw them at two, but that doesn't count — I don't have the file. The first time I saw my mother's face again was on a cracked phone screen, her singing a birthday song off-key that wasn't anybody's birthday, just because I asked her to sing.

Sang badly exactly like I remembered. Exactly. I hung up and couldn't talk to anybody for a while.

One day I'll go there. Hug the two of them up close. For now it's a screen — and a screen is already so much more than I had for a long time.

---

The letters, and then the calls, always came with a name on the front.

It's not Jack. Kim gave me the name Jack, a long time later, and it stuck. But the name my mother writes at the top of every letter, the name she shouts when she answers the call, only two people in the world still use that one. And both of them live in a dry place way up top.

I'm not going to tell you what it is. You didn't ask.

---

And this is where the story closes, the same way it opened.

Today I'm fifteen. I've got a hustle that turns, a house, the Warrior, and two parents in the backcountry who call to check whether I'm dressed warm.

Nobody knows this. Nobody knows where my parents live. Nobody knows where I live. Nobody knows what Jack's life is like on the inside.

Not because I hide it. I don't hide anything. It's that nobody ever asked.

And it's all exactly the way it is — because that's how Jack chose it.
