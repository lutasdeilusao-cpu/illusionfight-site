# CHAPTER 14 — The Ceremony

It became a ceremony. It lasted almost two years.

Once a month, first Monday, those two climbed the Hill to get beat up by me. And I looked forward to it.

Let me tell you how it changed.

---

**First month.**

I thought it'd be quick. Two kids, half my age, no technique. It wasn't.

The two of them together were something else. Each one covering the other's hole, exactly like I'd seen in the park, only now turned on me. I took it seriously start to finish, and even so I walked out of that fight with a mark on my chin nobody had left me in years.

And something happened that caught me off guard.

It was fun.

I'd always fought for a reason. To survive, when I was fodder. To prove strength, when I was muscle. To dominate, later. Fighting always had a function. It never crossed my mind that a fight could just be... good. The way a pickup game of soccer is good. With those two it was like that from the very first time.

I didn't tell anybody. It's not the kind of thing the master of a district says out loud.

---

**Third month.**

The Kid had tamed the panther.

That wild style of his, putting his hands on the ground, started turning into something else — still low, still animal, but with purpose. He stopped just wanting to land something. He started choosing where to land it.

The green hair had learned to read my tells. Knew which way I was going from my back foot. He started calling my moves out loud before I threw them, just to piss me off, and the worst part is he got them right.

---

**Sixth month.**

It was the first time one of them got close to the knee.

The Kid slipped under one of my shots and hooked his leg around mine. If he'd had five more pounds on him, I'd have gone down. He didn't. I got my balance back and finished the fight the usual way.

But the two of them exchanged a look when it happened. That look of somebody who saw the door crack open.

---

**One year.**

By then it wasn't a secret to anybody.

The whole Hill knew. Everything was open to those two — the gates, the stairways, the rooftops. They went into places the cops never went, got close to me in a way nobody ever got, and nobody touched them, because everybody knew that was my thing.

On the first Monday of every month, the neighborhood kids came up to watch. Sat on the upper rooftop like they were going to a game.

And every time it ended the same way. The green hair dropped his arms first.

— Stop. That's enough for today.

And the Kid, no. The Kid never fell, never said "stop," never accepted it. He finished standing, bleeding, staring. Every time.

---

In those two years both of them got a lot better. A whole lot.

And I took every fight seriously — seriously for real, because they had a clear goal and worked for it: one of my knees on the ground. Even saying they didn't care about my respect, that's what they wanted. To prove they could handle it.

What they didn't know is that first Monday was the only day in my month that wasn't work. It wasn't a threat, wasn't a collection, wasn't a plan. It was just a good fight with two kids who weren't scared of anything.

I was going to miss that.

Only back then I didn't know yet that it was going to end.
