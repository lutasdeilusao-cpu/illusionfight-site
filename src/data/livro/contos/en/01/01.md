# CHAPTER 1 — The House

My name is Ryan.

What comes next happened before I entered your story. Before the hallway. Before the box ([main line, ch. 15](/historias/lutas-de-ilusao/capitulo-15)). I’ll start at the beginning, because that’s the only way it makes sense.

---

It’s a house.

My house. I won’t pretend to be modest: it’s an extremely wealthy house. There are wings I haven’t set foot in for months. There are employees whose names I never learned, because I wasn’t supposed to.

My father and mother built all of this. They aren’t famous — they both treat discretion like a religion. But they’re rich in a way the word can’t capture. Businesses all over the world. I know some of them. Most, I don’t.

— I already have enough money for my children. — My father always speaks with the same calm. — For my children’s children. And for their children. And even then, they won’t be able to spend everything I’ve earned.

He lets the sentence breathe. Then comes the part that matters to him.

— I don’t need you to make money, Ryan. I need you to be a clear representation of our class, of our superior caste. Of what we are and what we represent to this world.

I agree. Agreeing is cheaper.

---

My mornings begin at five.

Not because I wake up. Because I’m woken up. One of the employees knocks twice on the door and turns on the closet light before I open my eyes, and from then on the day is already moving without me.

My clothes have been chosen. Someone else laid them out the night before, in the order I’m supposed to put them on. Breakfast has already been decided — portion, schedule, what is allowed and what isn’t. A menu my mother approves every Monday and that I’ve never seen change.

I eat alone at a table far too long for one person.

---

Before leaving, I sit at the piano.

It isn’t my choice. It’s always Beethoven. One of the sonatas, whichever one has been marked on the stand. I play the opening movements with the technique years of private lessons left in my fingers, and I feel nothing.

My father walks down the hallway at exactly that time. On purpose. He believes that a son playing Beethoven at five-thirty in the morning is living proof of everything he has achieved — of the place where we belong.

I close the lid at the end of the movement. Never before.

---

The driver takes me to Elite.

The car leaves at six-forty. He doesn’t talk to me, and I’m grateful for that.

Elite Academy appears at the end of a tree-lined avenue the city maintains only because of the people who study there. A tall gate. A garden that works all year to look as though no one tends it.

Inside, I don’t have to explain anything to anyone.

Everyone I pass in the hallway is at my level, or close enough that the difference doesn’t show. The same car brands in the student parking lot. The same trips during the same weeks of the year. We recognize one another without ever having to talk about it.

When you live like that, the world becomes simple. Doors open before you reach them. People adjust their tone when they speak to you. You move through the days the way you cross a lobby: without bumping into anything.

*It’s simple.*

And even so — with the family name, the car, Beethoven at five-thirty — inside that place I’m nothing but a doormat.
